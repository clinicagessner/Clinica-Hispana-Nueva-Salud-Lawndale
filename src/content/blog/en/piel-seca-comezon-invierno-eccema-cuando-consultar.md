# Dry, Itchy Winter Skin: Eczema and When to Get Seen

Don Arturo works at a warehouse off Navigation, and every November the same thing happens: his knuckles split into tiny cracks that burn under the faucet, and at night his shins itch so badly that he scratches in his sleep. His wife calls it "the cold-weather dryness." Sometimes that's all it is. Other times it's eczema, and telling the two apart can save you a whole winter of itching.

## Why skin struggles when the temperature drops

Houston doesn't get brutal cold, but a handful of fronts is enough for your skin to notice. The outer layer of the skin works like a brick wall held together by a "mortar" of natural oils. When that wall loses water, it cracks, lets irritants in, and the itch begins. This time of year several things wear it down at once:

- **Dry air and the heater.** Cold air holds little moisture, and the heat running inside the house or the car pulls it down even further.
- **Very hot showers.** They feel great on a chilly morning, but hot water strips away the oils that protect the skin, especially in a long shower.
- **Scented soaps or harsh bar soap**, along with perfumed detergents and fabric softeners that stay in your clothes.
- **Washing your hands many times a day** or keeping them wet at work: kitchens, cleaning crews, construction, car washes.
- **Wool or synthetic fabrics** right against the skin, which scratch and trap sweat.

## Plain dry skin or eczema?

Simple dry skin looks dull, flakes a little and itches mildly. Eczema, called atopic dermatitis in medical terms, is skin inflammation that comes and goes in flares and tends to get worse in the cold. It isn't contagious and it isn't caused by poor hygiene. This table is a general guide, not a diagnosis:

| What you notice | Winter dry skin | Possible eczema | Possible infection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Look | Dull, with fine flakes | Red or darker patches, thick and rough | Swollen, warm area that keeps getting redder |
| Itch | Mild, better with moisturizer | Intense, worse at night | Often more pain than itch |
| Where | Legs, arms, hands | Inside elbows and knees, hands, neck, eyelids | Over a crack or a scratched spot |
| Other signs | None | Tiny fluid-filled bumps, skin thickened from scratching | Pus, yellow or honey-colored crusts, fever |

On brown skin, eczema often doesn't look red at all. It can look brown, purple or grayish, and it may leave darker or lighter spots as it heals. That's why texture and itch tell you more than color.

## Eczema shows up in adults too

Many people think eczema belongs to babies. It does usually start in childhood, but some adults carry it from those years and others get it for the first time later in life. It's more common in people who have asthma, hay fever, or relatives with those conditions.

In adults it shows up a lot on the **hands**, especially in people who work around water, chemicals or gloves for hours. It also appears on the neck, around the eyes and in skin folds. Stress, sweat and poor sleep make flares last longer.

The core problem is the **itch-scratch cycle**: the skin itches, you scratch, the skin gets damaged, it gets more inflamed and itches even more. Over time, a spot that gets scratched a lot turns thick and darker. Breaking that cycle is half the treatment.

In young children, eczema tends to appear on the cheeks, the scalp or behind the knees. The basic care below helps them too, but if your child can't sleep because of the itch or the skin is oozing, bring them in to be checked.

## Home care that actually makes a difference

These steps follow what general health resources like MedlinePlus recommend, and they work best when you do them every day, not only once the skin is already bad:

1. **Bathe in lukewarm water, not hot,** for about 5 to 10 minutes. Use a gentle fragrance-free cleanser only on the underarms, groin and feet; the rest of the body barely needs it.
2. **Pat yourself dry** with the towel instead of rubbing, and leave the skin a little damp.
3. **Moisturize within the first few minutes** after bathing, while the skin still holds water. A thick fragrance-free moisturizing cream protects more than a thin lotion. Reapply on your hands every time you wash them.
4. **Protect your hands at work and in the kitchen:** gloves for washing dishes or handling bleach, and cloth gloves when you head out into the cold morning.
5. **Wear cotton** next to the skin and keep wool or synthetic sweaters as an outer layer. Wash clothes with fragrance-free detergent and skip the fabric softener.
6. **Turn the heat down a notch** at home and, if you have one, run a humidifier in the bedroom at night, cleaning it often so it doesn't grow mold.
7. **Keep your nails short** and, if you scratch in your sleep, try cotton gloves at night. A cool compress on the area calms the itch without hurting the skin.

Avoid putting strong home remedies on cracked skin, such as rubbing alcohol, diluted bleach or scented mixtures: they burn, irritate and open the crack further.

## When it's time to come in

Book a visit or walk in to the clinic if you notice any of these:

- The skin **doesn't improve** after a couple of weeks of daily care, or the flares keep coming back.
- Cracks **split open and bleed**, especially on the fingers, knuckles or heels.
- There are signs of **infection**: pus, yellow or honey-colored crusts, oozing blisters, pain that keeps growing, warmth in the area, or fever.
- The itch **keeps you from sleeping** or is getting in the way of your job.
- Patches cover a large part of the body or are on the face and eyelids.
- You have **diabetes**: a crack on the foot can get infected without you feeling it. Very dry skin can also go along with [thyroid](/services/tiroides) problems, which is worth checking if you also feel tired or cold all the time.

## Emergency signs

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if a red area of skin spreads fast along with fever or chills, if you see red streaks moving up from a wound, if your face, lips or tongue swell, if you're short of breath, or if the person is confused or very weak.
