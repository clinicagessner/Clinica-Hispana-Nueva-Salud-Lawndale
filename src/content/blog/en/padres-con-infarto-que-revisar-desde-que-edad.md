# Parent Had a Heart Attack? What to Check and When

Ernesto was 51 when he got a stent after collapsing with chest pain at a construction site. His son Julio, now 34, tells it as a family story: "Dad's heart gave out, but he's fine now." What Julio had never asked himself is what that story has to do with his own blood work, his own blood pressure, and his kids.

Quite a lot, it turns out. A parent, sibling or child with a heart attack does not mean the same thing will happen to you, but it does change the conversation: what is worth checking, when to start and how often to repeat it. The clinic's medical team put this guide together for families in Lawndale and the East End who carry that kind of history and are not sure where to begin.

## Not every family history weighs the same

A grandfather who had a heart attack at 88 is not the same signal as a father who had one at 50. What matters most is **early heart disease** in a first-degree relative (parents, siblings, children). The U.S. National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) puts the line here: [a father or brother diagnosed before age 55, or a mother or sister diagnosed before 65](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/coronary-heart-disease/risk-factors).

Besides a heart attack, these also count:

- A stent, a cardiac catheterization or bypass surgery.
- Angina, meaning chest pain with exertion that led to treatment.
- Sudden, unexplained death in a young family member.
- A stroke at a young age.

More than one affected relative, or a very young age at diagnosis, raises the weight further. Your father's side and your mother's side both count.

## What you inherit, and what you learn at the kitchen table

Families share two things: genes and habits. Sometimes what gets passed down is a form of high cholesterol that is present from birth, called **familial hypercholesterolemia**. The CDC advises talking to your health care team about it if you or a relative have [LDL cholesterol over 190 mg/dL, especially with early heart attacks in the family](https://www.cdc.gov/heart-disease-family-history/about/index.html). People who have it usually find out only through a blood test, because it causes no symptoms.

Other times, what passes from parents to children is a way of living: smoking at home, soda with every meal, hard physical work with no checkups, the habit of skipping the clinic "until it hurts." That part is not written in your genes, and it can change. That is why family history is not a sentence. It is a reason to get checked earlier than everyone else.

## Five questions for the next family gathering

The most useful information is not online; it is at the Sunday table. Before your visit, ask your parents, aunts, uncles or older siblings:

1. **Who had the problem, and how old were they?** Even an approximate age is the detail that changes the plan most.
2. **What exactly happened?** Heart attack, stent, bypass, angina, stroke or sudden death.
3. **Did they have diabetes, high blood pressure or high cholesterol?** Were they being treated?
4. **Did they smoke?** A heart attack at 50 in a lifelong smoker is read differently from one in someone who never smoked.
5. **Were they ever told it ran in the family?** Some families already know about very high cholesterol at young ages.

Write the answers down on your phone. If your father has passed away or does not remember the details, whatever the family recalls still helps.

## What to check, and from what age

General U.S. recommendations set a starting age for each test. With early heart disease in the family, the medical team often moves those checks earlier or repeats them more often. The table sums up the idea; the exact schedule is fitted to you.

| What to check | General recommendation | With early heart disease in the family |
|---|---|---|
| Blood pressure | At any visit, starting young | Measure it regularly and write it down; do not wait to feel something |
| Lipid panel (LDL, HDL, triglycerides) | Every 4 to 6 years from age 20 | From 20 without fail and, if it comes back abnormal, with closer follow-up |
| Glucose and A1C | From 35, or earlier with overweight | Earlier, especially if diabetes also runs in the family |
| Weight and waist size | At every checkup | Same, since belly fat adds to the risk |
| Electrocardiogram (EKG) | When there are symptoms or an exam requires it | The medical team decides whether it makes sense for you, especially after 40 with other risk factors |

The clinic does [blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre) including a lipid panel, glucose and A1C, as well as the [electrocardiogram](/services/electrocardiograma), which records the heart's electrical activity in a few minutes, painlessly. For the lipid panel and glucose, come in after 8 to 12 hours of fasting, drinking only water.

A normal EKG is good news, but it does not rule out arteries that are slowly narrowing. It does not replace blood work or blood pressure readings; it is one more piece of the puzzle.

## If your results come back abnormal

Finding high cholesterol or rising blood pressure at 35 is exactly the point: it buys you time to act. What usually follows is a [diabetes, hypertension and cholesterol care](/services/condiciones-cronicas) plan, with goals set for your age and family history, eating changes that respect Latino home cooking and, if the medical team considers it necessary, medication. When there is a prescription, it is sent electronically to the pharmacy you choose.

Two warnings the medical team gives often:

- **Do not copy your father's treatment.** What he was prescribed after his heart attack fits his case, not yours.
- **If you already take pills for blood pressure or cholesterol, do not stop them or change the dose on your own.** Talk to the medical team before changing anything, even if you feel perfectly well.

When the numbers or the family story call for it, the medical team can point you toward a specialized evaluation or tests done outside the clinic.

## The men in the family go first

In many Hispanic families, men are the ones who get checked least, and for them the family-history line comes earlier: before 55. If your father or brother had a heart attack that young, do not wait until 40 for your first checkup. The [men's health](/services/salud-hombre) service covers glucose, cholesterol, triglycerides and blood pressure in the same visit.

Women are not off the hook: risk goes up after menopause, and a mother or sister with a heart attack before 65 is just as important a warning.

## And your children

If very high cholesterol or young heart attacks run in your family, ask the medical team whether your kids need a blood test earlier than usual. Inherited high cholesterol can be found in childhood, and eating better and staying active from an early age makes the difference the previous generation did not get.

## What cannot wait for an appointment

No checkup helps if an emergency is ignored. Call 911 if you or someone in your family has:

- Severe chest pain, pressure or burning, especially if it spreads to the arm, jaw or back.
- Sudden shortness of breath, cold sweat, nausea or intense dizziness.
- Signs of a stroke: one side of the mouth twisting when they smile, an arm they cannot lift, or words that suddenly come out jumbled.

Do not drive yourself and do not come to the clinic: the ambulance crew starts treatment on the way.
