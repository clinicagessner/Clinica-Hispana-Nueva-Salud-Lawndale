# Kids' Weight: Soda, Screens and How to Talk About It

Mateo is 10, and he came to the clinic for the physical his school soccer team asked for. When he stepped on the scale, his mom Rosa went quiet. Driving back home through Lawndale, she told her sister what was really on her mind: "If I say something about his weight, he'll feel bad. And if I say nothing, who's going to tell him?"

A lot of families carry that question. A child's weight feels personal. It touches the food at home, the parents' work hours and even grandma's comments at the table. The clinic's medical team put this guide together to help you understand what is going on, which habits matter most and how to talk about it with your child and during the visit, without blame or scolding.

## Why a child's weight isn't read like an adult's

For an adult, the body mass index (BMI) is enough. For a child, that number shifts with age and sex, because the body is still growing. So it is compared against other kids of the same age on growth charts, and the result is given as a **percentile**.

These are the ranges the CDC uses from age 2 on:

| BMI-for-age percentile | What it means |
|---|---|
| Below 5 | Underweight |
| 5 to 84 | Healthy weight |
| 85 to 94 | Overweight |
| 95 or above | Obesity |

One number never tells the whole story. What says the most is the **curve**: if a child who was always around the 50th percentile climbs to the 85th in two years, that deserves a conversation even if he doesn't "look chubby" yet. There are also big-framed kids who grow steadily and are perfectly healthy.

## Soda and other drinks: the sugar you don't see

Often the change that helps most isn't on the plate but in the cup. One can of soda holds roughly 9 to 10 teaspoons of sugar, and the American Heart Association advises that children over 2 stay under about 6 teaspoons of added sugar for the whole day. Children under 2 shouldn't have added sugar at all.

The tricky part is that sugar hides in drinks that look harmless:

- **Boxed or bottled juice**, even the "100% fruit" kind: it carries the fruit's sugar without the fiber that fills you up.
- **Aguas frescas and horchata** made with lots of sugar, which in summer go down by the liter.
- **Sports and energy drinks**, marketed to teens as "for training."
- **Flavored milk and sweetened café con leche** at breakfast, next to the pan dulce.
- **Raspas and paletas** from the corner store, which turn into a daily after-school habit.

One trick that works in many East End homes: stop buying soda to keep in the fridge. If it isn't within reach, it becomes a party drink instead of an every-afternoon one. Plain water, water with cucumber or lime slices and unflavored milk are the everyday drinks.

## Screens: it's not just the sitting

Phones, tablets and video games affect weight in three ways. The first is obvious: hours without moving. The second is mindless eating, because kids snack more in front of a screen and lose track of how much. The third almost nobody notices: sleep. A child who falls asleep at midnight with the phone in bed gets less rest, and short sleep is linked to more hunger and more weight.

The AAP, the main children's health association in the United States, offers these general guidelines:

- Under 18 months: avoid screens, except video calls with family.
- Ages 2 to 5: no more than one hour a day of quality programs, ideally watched together.
- Age 6 and up: set clear, consistent limits, and make sure screens don't take hours away from sleep, active play or homework.

For kids and teens ages 6 to 17, the CDC recommends at least 60 minutes of physical activity every day. It doesn't have to be an organized sport: riding a bike around the block, playing soccer at the park or dancing in the living room all count.

## How to talk about weight at home

This was the part that worried Rosa most. These ideas help keep the conversation from hurting:

- **Talk about health and energy, not sizes.** "We want you strong enough to play the whole game" works better than "you're fat."
- **Change the rules for the whole family,** not just the child with more weight. If soda leaves the house, it leaves for everyone, Dad included.
- **Don't put your child on a diet yourself** or take meals away. In a growing body, that can cause harm and open the door to eating problems.
- **Skip nicknames and jokes,** even "loving" ones. A word like "gordito" at family gatherings can stick for years.
- **Ask how they feel.** Sometimes behind the weight there is teasing at school, sadness or stress, and that needs attention too.

## What happens at the visit

Many parents find out during a visit for something else, like the [school and sports physical](/services/examen-fisico-escolar). That exam records weight, height, body mass index and blood pressure, and checks the heart and lungs. It's a good moment to ask without fear: "How is his weight for his age?"

To get the most out of the visit, come with a few facts:

1. What your child drinks on a normal day, including juice and drinks at school.
2. How many hours they spend on screens and what time they fall asleep.
3. Whether they snore, get winded on the stairs or have knee pain.
4. Whether diabetes, high blood pressure or high cholesterol run in the family.

You can ask your child to step out for a moment if there's something you'd rather mention alone, or the other way around: teens often find it easier to talk with the medical team without parents in the room.

## When blood work comes in

The AAP suggests that children with obesity get blood work starting around age 10, because type 2 diabetes and high cholesterol already show up at those ages, especially in Hispanic families with a history of them. The clinic offers [blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre) that include glucose and cholesterol; the medical team decides which ones fit your child based on age, weight and family history.

One sign worth knowing is **dark, velvety patches on the neck or in the armpits**. Many parents think it's dirt, but it can mean the body is struggling with insulin. If there is a lot of tiredness, dry skin or the child isn't growing as expected, the medical team may also look at the [thyroid](/services/tiroides).

## Parents count too

Kids copy what they see at home. If you or your partner have diabetes, high blood pressure or high cholesterol that isn't under control, taking care of yourself also shapes the example you set. The [diabetes, hypertension and cholesterol management](/services/condiciones-cronicas) program supports the adults in the family, and if a medication is needed, the prescription is sent electronically to the pharmacy you choose.

## Signs that can't wait

Go to the emergency room or call 911 if your child is very thirsty and urinating a lot and is also vomiting, breathing fast or unusually drowsy: those can be signs of very high blood sugar. The same goes for a very bad headache with blurry vision or confusion.

Other signs aren't emergencies but do call for a visit soon: loud snoring with pauses in breathing during sleep, hip or knee pain that makes them limp, or a child who talks about skipping meals to lose weight.

## One step at a time

Rosa didn't change everything at once. She started by taking the soda out of the fridge and leaving the phones charging in the kitchen at nine at night. Six months later, at Mateo's next exam, his curve had stopped climbing.
