# Birth Control After Giving Birth and While Breastfeeding

Yesenia had her second baby girl in September. Between feedings every few hours, the exhaustion and her mother-in-law's long visit, birth control slipped to the bottom of the list. One afternoon, while rocking the baby to sleep, she wondered something many new moms ask quietly: "If my period hasn't come back and I'm breastfeeding, can I still get pregnant?" The short answer is yes, it can happen, which is why it pays to talk about a method before life settles back into its usual pace.

In this guide we walk you through when to start thinking about birth control after delivery, which kinds of methods are usually considered while nursing, and which questions to bring written down to your visit.

## Fertility can return before your period does

After childbirth, your body does not send a warning through your period when ovulation restarts. You ovulate before that first bleed, so a woman can become pregnant without having seen her period yet. For those who are not breastfeeding, fertility often comes back soon; for those who nurse, it tends to take longer, but every body keeps its own calendar and there is no way to guess the exact date.

That is why the general advice is to have a plan ready before you resume sex, rather than waiting for the first period to show up before making a decision.

## Does breastfeeding count as a method?

There is a natural method based on breastfeeding, but it only works when all three of these conditions are true at the same time:

- Your baby is **under six months old**.
- Breastfeeding is **exclusive or nearly exclusive**, day and night, with no long stretches between feedings and no regular formula or solid foods.
- Your **period has not returned** since the birth.

As soon as one of those three things changes, for example when the baby starts sleeping longer stretches, you go back to work and pump less often, or formula bottles come into the routine, protection drops sharply. Many Houston moms return to their jobs within a few weeks, and that is the moment to have another method already chosen.

## Which kinds of methods usually come up at the visit

The choice is made together with the medical team, based on your health, how the delivery went, whether you breastfeed and your plans for more children. The Nueva Salud Lawndale [birth control](/services/anticonceptivos) page explains that the visit includes checking your blood pressure, whether you smoke, your age, migraines, any history of blood clots and whether you are breastfeeding. With that information, these are the families of methods usually put on the table:

- **Barrier methods.** Condoms don't affect your milk or your hormones and can be used as soon as you resume sex. They also work as a backup while another method gets started.
- **Progestin-only methods.** These contain no estrogen. They include the minipill, the shot and the arm implant, which are generally considered compatible with breastfeeding.
- **IUD.** A long-acting method placed inside the uterus. If you are interested, the team guides you on where to get it placed and what to check first.
- **Methods with estrogen.** Combined pills, the patch or the ring are usually postponed during the first weeks after delivery, especially if you breastfeed, because estrogen can affect milk supply and the blood clot risk of that stage. When they can be considered is decided case by case.

No single kind of method is "the best" for everyone. A sleep-deprived mom who forgets things may do better with something that doesn't depend on remembering every day; another may prefer something she can stop as soon as she wants to try for another baby.

## When to think about a method

Ideally, the conversation starts during pregnancy, so the decision doesn't arrive in a hurry. Some women leave the hospital with a method already in place; others would rather wait until they feel recovered. Both choices are fine.

If you have already given birth and haven't decided anything yet, these are good moments to bring it up:

- Before you and your partner resume sex.
- When the baby starts eating something besides breast milk or sleeping through longer stretches.
- Before going back to work, if that will change the feeding rhythm.
- If your period has already returned, even if it is irregular.

If at any point you wonder whether you might already be pregnant again, a urine [pregnancy test](/services/prueba-embarazo) at the clinic gives the result in a few minutes and clears up the doubt before you start any hormonal method.

## What to ask at your visit

Having your questions saved on your phone helps a lot, especially when you walk in with the baby in your arms and a thousand things on your mind. Here are some worth asking:

1. Will this method affect my milk supply or my baby?
2. When does it start protecting me, and do I need condoms in the meantime?
3. How will my bleeding change? Some progestin methods make periods irregular or stop them for a while.
4. What if I want another pregnancy soon? How long does fertility take to return once I stop?
5. Do I have high blood pressure, migraines or a history of clots that changes my options?
6. How often should I come back for follow-up?
7. If I got an implant at the hospital and it doesn't suit me, can it be taken out?

Also bring the name of any medication or vitamin you are taking and, if you have it, your hospital discharge paper with the date and type of delivery. If you had a C-section, mention it: it is part of the history the team reviews.

## Warning signs: don't wait for a visit

Call 911 or go to the emergency room if, after delivery or at any other time, you have:

- Very heavy bleeding that soaks through pads quickly and comes with dizziness or fainting.
- Intense pelvic pain that comes on suddenly.
- A high fever together with pain in your lower belly.
- Sharp pain low on just one side of your belly, or any bleeding, while you think a new baby could be on the way or a home test already came out positive.

## A decision that can change over time

The method that suits you with a newborn doesn't have to be the same one when your baby turns one. You can start with something simple while you adjust to the new routine and switch later, once you know whether you want to wait several years or are hoping for a sibling soon. What matters is that the decision is yours, informed and on time.
