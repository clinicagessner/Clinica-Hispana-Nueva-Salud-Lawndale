# Your Ultrasound Report: Confusing Words, Explained

Rosa walked out of her pelvic ultrasound holding a printed page with one line circled in pen: "simple anechoic cyst, right ovary." She spent the night scrolling through websites, each one scarier than the last, and arrived at her follow-up visit sure that something was seriously wrong. The explanation she got was far calmer than anything she had pictured.

That story is common. An ultrasound report is written for another health professional, not for the patient, and it is full of terms that sound odd or even frightening in everyday language. The clinic's medical team wrote this glossary for patients in Lawndale and the East End who get their report and want to understand the language it is written in.

One warning first, and it matters more than anything else here: **this article helps you translate words, not interpret your result.** The same term can be perfectly normal for one person and call for follow-up in another, depending on age, symptoms, earlier scans and which organ is being described. What your finding means is something the medical team explains with your case in front of them.

## How a report is put together

Most reports follow the same order, and simply knowing it takes away half the worry:

- **Patient details and reason for the scan.** Why it was ordered, such as "right-sided pain" or "pregnancy follow-up."
- **Technique.** Which kind of ultrasound was done: through the abdomen, transvaginal, thyroid, and so on.
- **Findings.** An organ-by-organ description of what was seen and how big it is. This is where most of the jargon lives.
- **Impression or conclusion.** The summary in a few lines. If you read only one section, read this one, then still bring it to your visit so someone can walk you through it.

## Brightness words: how each tissue looks on screen

Ultrasound sends sound waves into the body and builds a black-and-white picture from how strongly they bounce back. That is why reports describe things by how bright they look. These four words show up on nearly every scan:

| Word on the report | What it looks like | What it usually describes |
|---|---|---|
| Anechoic | Black, no echoes | Fluid, like urine in the bladder or the inside of a simple cyst |
| Hypoechoic | A darker gray than its surroundings | Tissue that is softer or holds more water than its neighbor |
| Isoechoic | The same gray as nearby tissue | Something with a texture similar to what is around it |
| Hyperechoic or echogenic | Whiter, brighter | Denser tissue, fat, calcium or stones |

None of these words means "good" or "bad" by itself. Think of them as color adjectives. "Echogenic," which worries plenty of people, only says that an area sends back more echo than the tissue next to it.

## Shape words: cyst, nodule, mass

**Simple cyst.** A thin-walled pocket of fluid that looks smooth and black on the screen. It is one of the most frequent things an ultrasound picks up, whether in an ovary, a kidney, the liver or under the skin, and it often causes no trouble. The word "simple" describes its appearance: no walls or solid bits inside.

**Complex or complicated cyst.** A cyst that holds more than plain fluid, with inner walls, echoes or a thick lining. That does not make it serious, but it does mean the medical team will look at it more closely and may suggest another check.

**Nodule.** A small, roughly round lump of tissue that looks different from what surrounds it. It is a description, not a diagnosis. Thyroid nodules, for instance, are very common and most are harmless; the report records size, shape, edges and brightness so the team can decide whether it should be watched.

**Mass or lesion.** Broad terms reports use for "something taking up space" before anyone knows what it is. They sound heavy, yet in technical writing they carry no emotional weight. They just mean there is something to describe.

**Measurements in mm or cm.** Three numbers in a row (say, 18 × 14 × 12 mm) are length, width and height. They matter most for comparing with past or future scans, so keep every report.

## Words you'll see on specific scans

The clinic performs abdominal, pelvic and transvaginal, pregnancy, thyroid, and soft-tissue and renal ultrasounds. Here are a few terms typical of each.

**Abdominal and kidney**

- **Lithiasis or calculus:** a stone. "Cholelithiasis" means gallstones; "nephrolithiasis" means kidney stones.
- **Acoustic shadowing:** the dark band behind something hard, like a stone, because sound cannot pass through it. It helps confirm that what is seen is solid.
- **Biliary sludge:** thickened bile inside the gallbladder that settles like sediment.
- **Increased liver echogenicity:** the liver looks brighter than expected. The medical team reads this description alongside your lab work and history.
- **Hydronephrosis:** a kidney that is swollen because urine is not draining well. It can be mild or significant, and the report states the grade.

**Pelvic and transvaginal**

- **Follicle:** a normal small sac in the ovary where an egg matures each month.
- **Corpus luteum:** what remains of the follicle after ovulation; it is sometimes described as a cyst and is part of the normal cycle.
- **Fibroid or leiomyoma:** a very common growth of the uterine muscle.
- **Endometrium:** the inner lining of the uterus. Its thickness is listed, and it changes through the cycle.
- **Free fluid in the cul-de-sac:** a little fluid behind the uterus. A small amount can be normal after ovulation.

**Pregnancy**

- **Gestational sac:** the pocket where the pregnancy grows, and the first thing seen.
- **CRL (crown-rump length):** the embryo's length from head to bottom, used to estimate how many weeks along you are.
- **FHR:** fetal heart rate, meaning the heartbeat.

A pregnancy ultrasound adds to prenatal care; it never takes its place. Heavy bleeding, severe pain or fainting mean going to the emergency room or calling 911 right away, without waiting for a scan.

**Thyroid and soft tissue**

- **Lipoma:** a soft fatty lump under the skin.
- **Reactive-appearing lymph node:** a node that looks the way nodes usually do when the body is fighting a nearby infection.

## Closing phrases that raise the most questions

- **"Within normal limits" or "unremarkable":** what was seen looks as expected. In reports, "unremarkable" is a compliment.
- **"Incidental finding":** something spotted while looking for something else. Many need nothing beyond a note in your chart.
- **"Clinical correlation recommended":** the author asks that the result be read together with your symptoms and labs. It is routine wording, not an alarm bell.
- **"Follow-up in X months":** a repeat scan is suggested to see whether anything changes. A finding that stays the same over time is usually reassuring.

## Getting the most from the visit where results are explained

At the clinic, your report is explained to you in Spanish or English. To make that conversation count:

1. **Bring the full report**, plus earlier scans if you have them, so sizes can be compared.
2. **Underline every word you didn't understand**, the way Rosa did, and ask about each one.
3. **Ask three key questions:** Does this explain my symptoms? Does it need follow-up? When, and with what test?
4. **Ask what happens next.** If your case calls for a study the clinic doesn't perform, such as a vascular Doppler, the medical team helps arrange the referral.

When a finding involves the [thyroid](/services/tiroides), the ovaries or uterus through [gynecology](/services/ginecologia), or [blood tests](/services/examenes-sangre) that help make sense of it, the same clinic can keep the workup going.

## Don't wait for a report if…

Some symptoms leave no time for a glossary: very severe belly pain, high fever with pain, vomiting that won't stop, heavy bleeding or fainting. Any of these is a reason to head straight to the ER or dial 911 instead of reading on.
